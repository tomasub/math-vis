Preface
These lecture notes cover basics of linear algebra from the standpoint
of matrix computations: general vector spaces and linear maps are
not in the focus of attention, but the primary objects of interest are
matrices and (column) vectors that can directly be given as inputs
to a computer. The students are expected to have basic knowledge
about matrices; for example, the material presented during any of
the courses MS-A00XX Matrix Algebra is sufficient. Many of the
required fundamental tools are also reviewed in these notes.
    The three Chapters 1, 2 and 3 correspond, respectively, to the
three main themes of the lectures:

  1. Solvability and stability of linear systems Ax = b.

  2. Eigenvalue problems Av = λv and their fundamental applica-
     tions.

  3. Least squares problems, their geometric interpretation and re-
     lated matrix decompositions.

For simplicity and notational convenience, Chapters 1 and 3 consider
real vectors and matrices, even though the extension to the complex
case would be straightforward. However, Chapter 2 touches upon
the complex extension that cannot be avoided in the treatment of
eigenvalue problems.
    Matrices and subspaces are denoted by capital letters (e.g., A or
E), vectors by bolded lower case letters (e.g., x or b) and scalars
by standard lowercase letters (e.g., α or aij ). In particular, the
components of a vector x ∈ Rn are xj , j = 1, . . . , n. However,
the zero scalar and the zero vector of Rn are both denoted simply
as 0. The Cartesian basis vectors for Rn are e1, e2, . . . , en and the
identity matrix is denoted as

                    I := e1, e2, . . . , en ∈ Rn×n.

their column vectors. The positive integers n and m are reserved for

1.1      Solvability of linear systems
The main aim of this section is to study the existence and uniqueness
of a solution to (1.1). These properties can be characterised in terms
of the nullspace and the range of the matrix A. In the following,
we will denote the columns of A by a1, a2, . . . , an ∈ Rm (cf. (1.2)).

Definition 1.1.1. Let A ∈ Rm×n. The range of A is the set

        R(A) = y ∈ Rm | y = Ax for some x ∈ Rn
               
                                        m              (1.5)
             = span(a1, . . . , an) ⊂ R

and the nullspace of A is the set

                N(A) = {x ∈ Rn | Ax = 0} ⊂ Rn.




                                  8
  The sets R(A) and N(A) are closed under addition and scalar
multiplication. Indeed, if x, y ∈ N(A), then

                A(αx + βy) = αAx + βAy = 0,

meaning that also αx + βy ∈ N(A) for any α, β ∈ R.

   Similarly, if x, y ∈ R(A), i.e. x = Az and y = Aw for some
z, w ∈ Rn, then

           A(αz + βw) = αAz + βAw = αx + βy,

i.e., αx + βy ∈ R(A) for any α, β ∈ R.

   This means that the subsets R(A) ⊂ Rm and N(A) ⊂ Rn are
actually subspaces.

Definition 1.1.2. Let E ⊂ Rn be such that for any x, y ∈ E
and α ∈ R
                x + y ∈ E and αx ∈ E.
Then E is called a subspace of Rn. (In particular, Rn itself as
well the trivial subspace {0} are subspaces of Rn.)

                                 10
   The uniqueness of a solution is studied via the homogeneous prob-
lem: Find y ∈ Rn such that

                   Ay = 0     ⇐⇒     y ∈ N(A).

If the homogeneous equation has only the trivial solution y = 0, a
solution to the linear system (1.1) is unique (if one exists).

Lemma 1.1.1. Assume that (1.1) has a solution. The solution
is unique if and only if N(A) = {0}.

Proof. Let x ∈ Rn be a solution of (1.1). Assume that N(A) is
nontrivial, i.e., there exists 0 ̸= z ∈ N(A). Then,

                A(x + z) = Ax + Az = Ax = b,

meaning that we have constructed a second, distinct solution x+z ̸=
x.
   This proves the ‘only if’ part of the claim.




                                11
   In order to prove the ‘if part’, assume that N(A) = {0} and let
both x1, x2 ∈ Rn be solutions to (1.1), i.e.,

                    Ax1 = b and Ax2 = b.

Subtracting the two equations and linearity of A give

                         A(x1 − x2) = 0.

Hence, x1 − x2 ∈ N(A), which means by assumption that x1 − x2 =
0, and thus all solutions to (1.1) must be the same.

   Let us rephrase our conclusions for square matrices, i.e., m = n.

By the definition of R(A), the problem

                              Ax = b.                           (1.11)

has at least one solution for all b ∈ Rn if and only if R(A) = Rn if
and only if rank(A) = dim R(A) = n.

According to Dimension Theorem,

             dim N(A) = n − dim R(A) = n − n = 0.

Thus, N(A) = {0}, and the solution of (1.11) is also unique.

   Conversely, the solution to (1.11) is unique if and only if dim N(A) =
0, which implies dim R(A) = n by the Dimension Theorem 1.1.1.
Then (1.11) is solvable for all b ∈ Rn.

Conclusion for square matrices A: The solvability of (1.11) for
all b ∈ Rn and the uniqueness of a solution to (1.11) are equivalent
conditions.

1.2      Norms and inner products
To study the stability of the solution to (1.1) with respect to pertur-
bations, inaccuracies or uncertainties in b and A, we need tools for
measuring the ‘size’ of a vector or a matrix. We use (vector) norms
for this purpose.

Further, orthogonality notions are studied in terms of inner products.
Inner products may be used for defining norms, but not all norms
can be produced this way. Finally, all inner products in Rn are
characterised in terms of symmetric n × n matrices.

1.2.1     Vector norm
The size (length) of a vector x ∈ Rn is measured by a (vector) norm.

There are many different vector norms.

Definition 1.2.1. A function ∥ · ∥ : Rn → R is a norm if it
satisfies


   i. ∥x∥ ≥ 0 for all x ∈ Rn and ∥x∥ = 0 if and only if x = 0,

  ii. ∥x + y∥ ≤ ∥x∥ + ∥y∥ for all x, y ∈ Rn,

 iii. ∥αx∥ = |α|∥x∥ for all x ∈ Rn and α ∈ R.

   Definition 1.2.1 characterises a reasonable measure of length.

The norms used in linear algebra can be divided into generic norms
and problem specific norms.

Much of the linear algebra theory relates to generic norms that
plainly satisfy Definition 1.2.1, nothing more.

Problem specific norms are used when some practical application
requires a particular way of measuring vector length.

Many times a given problem becomes much easier to solve if “the
right kind of norm” is used in its solution. It often takes a certain
amount of cleverness and experience to come up with “the right kind
of norm”, though.

easy. As it is not, and we have to deal with it.

Let V be a vector space and ∥ · ∥ a norm on it. Recall that (V, ∥ · ∥)

In a normed vector space, a sequence {xj }j∈N is called Cauchy
sequence if for each ϵ > 0 there exists N = Nϵ ∈ N such that

              min {j, k} > Nϵ     ⇒    ∥xj − xk ∥ < ϵ.


In general normed vector spaces, some Cauchy sequences converge
to a limit vector
                           x = lim xj
                                 j→∞

and some others do not.

However, any convergent sequence is a Cauchy sequence in any normed
vector space.




                                 67
   The convergence is here understood in the sense of Weierstrass:
For all ϵ > 0 there exists M = Mϵ ∈ N such that

                   j > Mϵ     ⇒    ∥xj − x∥ < ϵ.


We say that the normed vector space V is complete if all Cauchy
sequences converge to a limit. In other words, there are no non-
convergent Cauchy sequences in a complete normed vector space.

Complete normed vector spaces are called Banach spaces. If their
norms are defined in terms of inner products, they are called Hilbert
spaces.

It can be shown that any finite-dimensional vector space V
equipped with any vector norm ∥ · ∥ is a Banach space.

In particular, (R, | · |) is a Banach space whereas (Q, | · |) is not.
This is known as the completeness of the real number system R.

1.4      Solving Ax = b on a computer
The Lecture Notes contain a section for solving a linear system

                              Ax = b

by computer using floating point arithmetics.

Computers are inherently unable to compute with general real num-
bers in R since they are finite (state) machines — not quite even Tur-
ing machines since the length of their “strip of tape” is bounded
by the physical storage capacity.

The finite accuracy of numerical floating point processors is given in
terms of the machine epsilon u which represents the size of the
smallest positive number that can be represented at all.

One way of intuitively understanding floating point numbers is to
consider each such number – not as a real number – but an interval
of real numbers of length u.

Floating point numbers are a number system of some kind. The
rules of computation differ from those of R, but unfortunately, not
favourably so.


                                 78
When considering perturbations δA and δb as above related to solv-
ing Ax = b, each of these perturbations may be due to the finite
accurary achieved by the floating point arithmetics.

Of course, the data A and b itself can be inherently inaccurate as
well. Theorem 1.3.2 applies then, too.

Moreover, in the course of computing x from Ax = b, e.g., elemen-
tary (Gaussian) row operators, by numerous additions and multipli-
cations, roundings take place at each step.

So, it is far from evident that computing a solution of Ax = b is
even in principle possible for reasonably large n × n matrices A.
A practical example involving Hilbert matrices is given in the
Lecture Notes, and it is a hopeless problem for n ≥ 15 using the
floating point precision of usual computers. At n = 15, we are far
from running out of computer memory. We run out of accuracy.

The student is invited to read Sections 1.4-1.4.1 in the Lecture Notes
independently. About the machine epsilon, a rather good article can
be found in English Wikipedia.

This is not a trivial matter.

We don’t do empty nonsense in mathematics, do we?

   Why complex numbers?

Even A ∈ R2×2 given by [ 01 −1
                             0 ] has eigenvalues ±i ∈ C. So there
is no way we can do satisfactory eigenvalue theory without using
complex numbers at all.

It is better develop the theory for complex valued vectors Cn and
matrices Cn×n throughout.

Eigenvalues of matrices are roots (zeroes) of a characteristic poly-
nomial. If matrix A is complex, the coefficients of the characteristic
polynomial pA(λ) are (typically) complex-valued. However, the com-
plex numbers C are an algebraically closed field — roots of all
polynomials with coefficients in C are in C.

So, we do not need yet another non plus ultra further extension of
C to deal with eigenvalues of A ∈ Cn×n.

2.2      Hermitian matrices
Hermitian matrices are encountered in numerous applications.


Definition 2.2.1. A matrix A ∈ Cn×n is Hermitian if A∗ = A.


Obviously, the diagonal elements of a Hermitian matrix must be real.

A real matrix is Hermitian if and only if it is symmetric:

                             AT = A.



Definition 2.2.2. A matrix U ∈ Cn×n is unitary if it is invert-
ible and U ∗ = U −1.


A real matrix is unitary if and only if it is orthogonal:

                            U T = U −1.

Proof. Let λ ∈ σ(A) and v ̸= 0 such that Av = λv.

                     v ∗Av = v ∗(λv) = λ∥v∥22.

2.3      Similarity transformations
Recall that the following are equivalent for any matrix n × n matrix
A ∈ Cn×n:

  1. A is diagonalisable.

  2. Matrix is non-defective, meaning that

                  µA(λ) = µG(λ)       for all   λ ∈ σ(A).

  3. A has n linearly independent eigenvectors.


For defective matrices A ∈ Cn×n one can still look for some other
decomposition of the form

                            A = SBS −1,                       (2.13)

where B is a more simple matrix than the original A in some
other sense.

These decompositions have great theoretical and practical value in
carrying out computations on matrices A.




                                119
Definition 2.3.1. The matrices A ∈ Cn×n and B ∈ Cn×n are
called similar if there exists an invertible S ∈ Cn×n such that

                          A = SBS −1.


Matrix similarity is an equivalence relation in the set of all n×n
matrices Cn×n:

  1. Reflexivity: A is similar with A itself.

  2. Symmetricity: A is similar with B if and only if B is similar
     with A.

  3. Transitivity: If A is similar with B and B is similar with C,
     then A is similar with C.


           Check that the above three conditions hold.

    For two similar matrices A and B, the powers of B could be much

  1. B is diagonal.

  2. B is upper or lower triangular; i.e., all elements below or above

  3. B has otherwise a lot of zero elements.


Two n×n matrices A and B are similar if and only if they represent
the same linear mapping, say T : V → V , in two different bases of
V with dim V = n.

If A = SBS −1 represents a linear mapping T in the natural Carte-

basis defined by the columns of S −1. Indeed, the columns of S −1 are
the vectors {S −1e1, S −1e2, . . . , S −1en}.

A diagonalisable matrix A is represented by a diagonal matrix Λ in
its own eigenbasis.

Further, we have seen that the eigenbasis can be chosen to be or-
thonormal if A is Hermitian.

3.5      Singular value decomposition
The singular value decomposition (SVD) is a matrix factori-
sation of the form
                      A = U ΣV T (= U ΣV ∗),
where U ∈ Rm×m and V ∈ Rn×n are orthogonal (resp., unitary)
matrices. The matrix Σ ∈ Rm×n is a non-square matrix with all
off-diagonal entries zero, and diagonal elements non-negative.

For a symmetric (or Hermitian) and positive definite matrix A, the
unitary diagonalisation
                       A = U ΛU ∗ = U ΛU −1
is a SVD with Σ = Λ and V = U .

If A is symmetric (Hermitian) but not non-negative, then some eigen-
value in Λ is negative. This sign problem can be easily dealt with to
produce SVD. How?

In fact, SVD can be formed for any matrix A by allowing V ̸= U
and non-square Σ. Before proving that, let us consider the nature
and some consequences of the SVD.

The matrices U and V in SVD are not unique, given A.

   Forming the SVD of A ∈ Rm×n requires finding the orthonormal
bases {u1, . . . , um} and {v 1, . . . , v n}.

the SVD A = U ΣV T .

                              A = U ΣV T

  2. the right-singular vectors {v 1, . . . , v p}, and

  3. the non-zero singular values σ1, . . . , σp.
